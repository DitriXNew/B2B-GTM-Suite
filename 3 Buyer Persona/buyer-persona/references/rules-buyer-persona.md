# Buyer Persona Rules — Detailed Reference

## Segment evaluation criteria

When choosing a segment, assess it together with the user:

- Is this segment growing?
- What condition is it in?
- Is all of the company's competition present there?
- Does the company have a strong value proposition for this segment?
- How long does the buying process take?
- What can go wrong? (risks, e.g. legal, seasonality)
- What do they buy, and at what average price?
- Can the company actually reach the people in this segment?
- How many companies are there in this segment on the market?

**At least one of the target segments should be growing.**

---

## The Pineapple rule — choosing segment breadth

Think of precision as a ladder: "pineapple" → "tropical fruit" → "fruit" → "food". Each step up is broader and vaguer; each step down is narrower and sharper.

**By default, suggest the broadest possible segment**, as long as that entire segment shares a problem for which the company has a strong value proposition.

Narrow the segment when:
- Conversion across the broad segment is too low
- The market is crowded and you need to stand out
- The company's values speak only to a narrow niche

Examples:
- OK: "B2B SaaS companies with 50–200 employees" — if the values address this entire segment
- Too narrow without reason: "HR-software vendors in a single city"
- Too broad: "All software companies" — if the values only address mid-market SaaS

**The level of precision should be no greater than necessary, but enough to build effective processes.**

---

## The difference: Problem vs Challenge

**Problem (hurts TODAY):**
- A sudden failure, a process error, a financial loss
- Requires immediate action
- The customer is actively looking for a solution
- Examples: "We're losing 15% of leads because the CRM isn't configured", "The production line stands idle 3h a day due to outdated tooling", "A key salesperson left and took the relationships with them"

**Challenge (doesn't hurt today, but we have to do it):**
- Market shifts, regulations, expansion plans
- No urgency, but ignoring it leads to losses
- The customer knows they should act, but keeps putting it off
- Examples: "We have to enter a new export market within 2 years", "ESG regulations will force process changes", "The competition is automating sales and we haven't yet"

---

## The non-obviousness rule for problems and challenges

Problems and challenges must NOT be only the obvious ones. "The CEO wants sales growth": everyone knows that, so everyone has already exploited it.

Look for problems:
- That no one in the industry targets the way the company plans to
- That are specific to the segment (not general-business)
- That stem from operational realities, not from wishful thinking

**Bad examples (obvious: everyone already says this):**
- "Wants to increase revenue"
- "Looking for savings"
- "Needs better quality"
- "Wants to grow faster"
- "Needs more leads"
- "Wants to cut costs"
- "Looking to improve efficiency"
- "Needs to stay competitive"
- "Wants happier customers"
- "Needs to be more innovative"

**Good examples (non-obvious: specific, operational, measurable):**
- "Loses orders because there's no quoting system, and salespeople spend 2h manually calculating each offer"
- "Turnover in the purchasing department means supplier knowledge is lost every six months"
- "The board requires sales reports, but the data is spread across 4 different Excel files"
- "Every new hire takes 3 months to ramp because all the process knowledge lives in one senior employee's head"
- "Production schedules are replanned by hand each morning, so a single rush order throws off the whole week"
- "Field technicians fill in paper forms, so invoicing lags 2–3 weeks behind the work actually done"
- "Marketing and sales argue over lead quality because no one agrees on what 'qualified' means"
- "The warehouse counts stock once a quarter, so they only discover shortages when an order can't ship"
- "Customer complaints come in by phone, email, and chat, and nothing gets logged, so the same issue recurs unnoticed"
- "Each branch uses its own pricing spreadsheet, so the same customer gets two different quotes"
- "Compliance reports are assembled manually before every audit, eating a full week of the finance team's time"
- "Returns are processed without root-cause tracking, so a recurring defect keeps shipping for months"

## Questions for extracting problems and challenges

### About problems (what hurts today)
- What is the biggest frustration in this person's daily work?
- Where do they waste time on things that should work automatically?
- What keeps them awake at night thinking about work?
- What errors or losses recur regularly?
- What would happen if they changed nothing over the next 12 months?

### About challenges (what they have to do)
- What market changes are forcing action?
- What goals has the board set for this year that require new tools or processes?
- What regulations or customer requirements are forcing changes?
- Where is the competition already ahead?

### About the personal trigger
- What has to happen for this person to start ACTIVELY looking for a solution?
- What situation makes them say "enough, I have to change this"?
- Is it a one-time event (e.g. losing a customer) or a growing problem?

### About objections (what blocks the purchase)
- What is this person afraid of with a purchase like this?
- What might they say to delay the decision?
- What bad experiences from the past might shape the decision?
- Who in the organization can block the purchase, and why?

### Probing
- How much does it cost the company per year? (turn the problem into money)
- How often does it happen?
- What have they tried before, and why didn't it work?

## Generating objections

Based on the problems and values you've collected, generate 3-5 objections per person. Each objection should have TWO variants:
- **Raw** — the way the customer actually says it (short, blunt): "Too expensive", "Not now", "I have to talk to my boss"
- **Expanded** — what the customer really means underneath: "I don't see how this will pay off given our budget"

### Raw objections (most common patterns)
- "Too expensive" / "We don't have the budget"
- "Not now" / "Maybe next quarter"
- "I have to check with someone" / "I don't decide alone"
- "We already tried it and it didn't work"
- "What if it doesn't work for us?"
- "We have more important priorities"
- "Our current supplier is OK"
- "It's too big a change"

### Expanded patterns per person type

**Person managing the company:**
- "We don't have the budget for this" -> Afraid to tie up cash without certainty of return
- "How can we be sure it will work for us?" -> Had bad experiences with previous suppliers
- "We have more important priorities" -> Doesn't yet see the scale of the problem

**Operational person / team manager:**
- "The rollout will paralyze our work" -> Afraid of transitional chaos
- "The team won't adapt" -> Knows people are resistant to change
- "The previous supplier promised the same thing" -> Lost trust in this type of solution

**Technical person / user:**
- "It doesn't integrate with our system" -> Afraid of extra work
- "I want to see it in action" -> Won't buy something they haven't tested
- "The specification doesn't meet the requirements" -> Looking for a reason to stick with the current solution

After generating them, ALWAYS validate with the user.

---

## Matching values to people

From the PSO (Problem · Solution · Outcome) list, assign values to people using this rule: **the same value can go to different people, but with a different emphasis (a different problem of that person).**

Matching guidelines:
- Person managing finances -> values affecting: cashflow, ROI, financial risk, cost of inaction
- Person managing operations -> values affecting: performance, time, reliability, simplicity
- Person managing the team -> values affecting: turnover, onboarding, competencies, motivation
- Technical person -> product values: parameters, certifications, compatibility, durability
- Purchasing person -> service values: payment terms, logistics, minimum order, warranty

Don't assign all values to all people. Pick the 3-7 strongest per person.

Cite each matched value by its ID (e.g. `PV-03`) so the persona stays linked to the value list.

## Persona validation methods (to recommend to the user)

A persona is a hypothesis: it has to be validated. Experiment methods:

1. Phone interview with current customers from this segment
2. Interview with customers reached e.g. on LinkedIn (deliberately, as research)
3. A value proposition sheet in PDF — sent to customers for their feedback
4. Prospecting campaign (email/phone) — testing the messaging
5. Landing page test with paid advertising — does the segment respond?
6. Analysis of Google search trends

**For key hypotheses — min. 2-3 different experiments.** Don't rely on just one.
